01

Methodology

Four sequential steps take raw accident coordinates to flagged anomalies.

STEP 1

Clean

Drop FARS sentinel values (99.9999° / 999.9999°) used to mark unreported GPS coordinates. Filter to Texas — the highest-volume state in the file.

STEP 2

Calibrate eps

Compute each point's 5th-nearest-neighbor distance (haversine). The k-distance elbow lands near 5 km, setting DBSCAN's neighborhood radius.

STEP 3

Cluster

Run DBSCAN (eps = 5 km, min_samples = 5, haversine metric) on latitude/longitude to form hotspots and separate out noise.

STEP 4

Flag anomalies

Z-score each cluster's accident count and fatality total. Clusters scoring above 2σ on either metric are flagged as anomalous.

02

Why DBSCAN

Accident locations don't arrive with a known number of hotspots, and they don't cluster into neat circles — they follow roads. That ruled out K-Means in favor of a density-based approach.

K-Means

  • Requires the number of clusters (k) to be fixed in advance
  • Forces spherical, evenly-sized partitions
  • Every point is assigned to a cluster — no concept of noise

DBSCAN

  • Discovers the number of clusters directly from density
  • Traces irregular shapes that follow real road corridors
  • Isolates sparse, non-recurring accidents as noise by design

06

Conclusion

Density-based clustering recovers Texas's major accident concentrations directly from raw GPS data, with no need to predefine how many hotspots exist. Layering a statistical anomaly check on top separates ordinary hotspots from the small number that are genuine outliers — Houston, Dallas–Fort Worth, and San Antonio, in this run. Applied at city or corridor scale, the same pipeline gives traffic authorities a repeatable, data-driven way to prioritize road-safety interventions instead of relying on manual review.
